## How does the Calculator work?

The core engine of our calculator relies on the unbreakable rule of algebra: if you have an equation with three variables (a, b, c), and you know the value of two of them, you can isolate and solve for the third.

The calculator has two primary modes of operation, depending on what information you are missing:

### Mode 1: Finding the Hypotenuse (Missing 'c')
If you input the lengths of the two smaller legs ('a' and 'b'), the calculator executes the classic formula: **c = √(a² + b²)**.
It squares 'a', squares 'b', adds those two massive numbers together, and then takes the square root of that total sum to output the long hypotenuse.

### Mode 2: Finding a Missing Leg (Missing 'a' or 'b')
If you input the length of the massive hypotenuse ('c') and one of the smaller legs (let's say 'b'), the calculator instantly rewrites the algebraic formula to isolate the missing side: **a = √(c² - b²)**.
It squares the big hypotenuse, squares the smaller leg, *subtracts* the smaller leg from the hypotenuse, and takes the square root of that difference to output the missing leg.

## Manual Calculation Methods (Formulas)

While the calculator handles everything in milliseconds, understanding how to do the manual algebra is critical for passing geometry exams. Let's walk through the manual process for both scenarios.

### Scenario 1: Finding the Hypotenuse (c)
You are building a ramp. The base of the ramp on the ground is 4 feet long (a). The height of the ramp is 3 feet tall (b). How long is the actual slanted wooden ramp board (c)?

*   **Formula:** a² + b² = c²
*   **Step 1 (Plug in numbers):** 4² + 3² = c²
*   **Step 2 (Square them):** 16 + 9 = c²
*   **Step 3 (Add them):** 25 = c²
*   **Step 4 (Take Square Root):** √25 = c
*   **Answer:** **c = 5 feet**. 
*(This specific 3-4-5 ratio is incredibly famous in construction, known as a Pythagorean Triple).*

### Scenario 2: Finding a Missing Leg (a or b)
You have a 13-foot ladder (c). You lean it against a wall. The bottom of the ladder is resting exactly 5 feet away from the base of the wall (a). Exactly how high up the wall does the top of the ladder reach (b)?

*   **Formula:** a² + b² = c²
*   **Step 1 (Plug in numbers):** 5² + b² = 13²
*   **Step 2 (Square them):** 25 + b² = 169
*   **Step 3 (Isolate b² by subtracting 25 from both sides):** b² = 169 - 25
*   **Step 4 (Subtract):** b² = 144
*   **Step 5 (Take Square Root):** √144 = b
*   **Answer:** **b = 12 feet**. The ladder reaches exactly 12 feet up the wall.

## Common Mistakes Users Make

When students and amateur builders attempt these calculations manually, they frequently fall into these algebraic traps:

1.  **Using it on Non-Right Triangles:** This is a fatal error. The Pythagorean Theorem `a² + b² = c²` ONLY works if the triangle has a perfect 90-degree corner. If the triangle is squished or stretched (acute or obtuse), the formula mathematically breaks down. You must use Trigonometry (the Law of Cosines) for those shapes.
2.  **Misidentifying the Hypotenuse:** The hypotenuse ('c') is *always* the longest side, and it is *always* the slanted side directly opposite the 90-degree corner. If you accidentally plug the massive hypotenuse number into the 'a' or 'b' slot, your entire equation will be ruined. 
3.  **Forgetting to take the Square Root:** This is the most common homework mistake. A student will write `3² + 4² = c²`, then write `9 + 16 = 25`. They will proudly circle "25" as the answer. But 25 is `c²`, not `c`. You must take the square root of 25 to find the true length of 5. If your answer seems comically massive compared to the other two sides, you forgot the final square root step.
4.  **Squaring Errors:** Remember that squaring a number means multiplying it by itself, NOT multiplying it by 2. `5²` means 5 × 5 (which is 25). Students rushing through exams will often write `5 × 2 = 10`, completely destroying the equation.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

**1. What is a "Pythagorean Triple"?**
A Pythagorean Triple is a specific set of three whole numbers that fit the theorem perfectly, without any messy decimals. The most famous is 3-4-5 (3² + 4² = 5²). Another common one is 5-12-13. Builders memorize these triples so they can square walls without needing a calculator.

**2. Can the hypotenuse ever be shorter than one of the legs?**
No. It is a fundamental law of geometry that the hypotenuse (c) is absolutely always the longest side of a right triangle. If your calculation results in a hypotenuse that is shorter than 'a' or 'b', you have made a severe arithmetic error.

**3. Does it matter which leg is 'a' and which leg is 'b'?**
No, it does not matter at all. Because addition is commutative (3+4 is the same as 4+3), you can assign the vertical leg to 'a' and the horizontal leg to 'b', or vice versa. The math will work out exactly the same. Only 'c' (the hypotenuse) has a strict, unchangeable placement.

**4. Who actually invented the Pythagorean Theorem?**
While it is named after the Greek mathematician Pythagoras (who popularized it in 500 BC), historical evidence shows that ancient Babylonian and Indian mathematicians understood and used this mathematical relationship over a thousand years before Pythagoras was even born!

**5. How is this theorem used in GPS navigation?**
When your phone calculates the distance between two GPS coordinates, it treats the difference in Longitude as leg 'a' and the difference in Latitude as leg 'b'. It then uses the Pythagorean theorem to calculate the hypotenuse, which is the direct, straight-line distance between the two points.

**6. What is the Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem?**
The converse simply runs the logic backwards. It states: If you have a random triangle, and you square the three sides, and `a² + b²` exactly equals `c²`, then you have mathematically proven that the triangle has a perfect 90-degree right angle. (This is exactly what the carpenter does in Scenario A).

**7. Can any of the sides be a negative number?**
No. Because the theorem deals with physical geometry and the lengths of physical objects, a negative length is impossible. The numbers plugged into the calculator must always be positive.
