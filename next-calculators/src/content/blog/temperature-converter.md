## How does the Calculator work?

Unlike length or weight conversions (which only require you to multiply by a single factor like 2.2), temperature conversion is algebraically complex because the different scales do not start at the same "Zero" point. 

Here is exactly how the calculator processes your data behind the scenes:

### Step 1: Celsius to Fahrenheit
If you ask the calculator to convert 20°C into Fahrenheit:
The calculator multiplies the Celsius temperature by **9/5** (or 1.8). This accounts for the fact that a Fahrenheit degree is smaller than a Celsius degree. (20 × 1.8 = 36).
It then algebraically adds **32**. This accounts for the offset starting points (since 0°C is equal to 32°F). (36 + 32 = 68). (Output: 20°C = 68°F).

### Step 2: Fahrenheit to Celsius
If you ask the calculator to convert 100°F into Celsius:
The calculator algebraically subtracts **32** to align the zero points. (100 - 32 = 68).
It multiplies the remainder by the fraction **5/9**. (68 × 5/9 = 37.777...).
It formats the output seamlessly with decimal precision. (Output: 100°F = 37.77°C).

### Step 3: The Kelvin Translation
Kelvin and Celsius have the exact same degree size; their only difference is the starting point. To convert Celsius to Kelvin, the calculator simply adds **273.15**. To convert Kelvin to Celsius, it subtracts 273.15.

### The Core Algebraic Formulas

1.  **Celsius to Fahrenheit:** F = (C x 1.8) + 32
2.  **Fahrenheit to Celsius:** C = (F - 32) x 5/9
3.  **Celsius to Kelvin:** K = C + 273.15
4.  **Kelvin to Celsius:** C = K - 273.15

### The "Mental Math" Shortcut for Travel
If you are standing in a foreign country and just need a rough estimate of the weather without opening a calculator, use this famously fast (but slightly inaccurate) shortcut:
*   **C to F Shortcut:** Multiply by 2 and add 30. (e.g., 20°C × 2 = 40. 40 + 30 = 70°F. The actual answer is 68°F, but 70 is close enough to know you should wear a t-shirt).
*   **F to C Shortcut:** Subtract 30 and divide by 2. (e.g., 90°F - 30 = 60. 60 / 2 = 30°C. The actual answer is 32.2°C, but 30 is close enough to know it's a hot beach day).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

**1. Why does the United States still use Fahrenheit?**
Daniel Fahrenheit invented the first reliable mercury thermometer in the 1700s, and the British Empire heavily adopted it. When the British Empire transitioned to the Metric system (Celsius) in the 20th century, the USA was too deeply industrialized to easily switch. Culturally, many Americans prefer Fahrenheit for weather because 0°F to 100°F beautifully maps to the extremes of human survivability. 

**2. What does 0° Fahrenheit actually represent?**
While 0°C is the freezing point of pure water, Daniel Fahrenheit set his 0°F mark based on the freezing point of a highly specific, super-chilled mixture of water, ice, and ammonium chloride (a type of salt). 

**3. What is Absolute Zero?**
Absolute Zero (0 Kelvin, or -273.15°C) is the theoretical coldest temperature possible in the universe. At this temperature, all atomic and molecular kinetic movement completely stops. It is physically impossible to reach a temperature lower than 0 K.

**4. How hot is the Sun in Celsius?**
The surface of the Sun is roughly 5,500 °C. The core of the Sun, where thermonuclear fusion occurs, is an incomprehensible 15,000,000 °C (15 Million degrees).

**5. Is Centigrade the same thing as Celsius?**
Yes. "Centigrade" (meaning a 100-degree scale) was the original name of the Celsius scale. In 1948, the international scientific community officially renamed it to Celsius to honor its inventor, Anders Celsius. Older generations still occasionally use the term Centigrade.

**6. What is the Rankine Scale?**
Rankine is to Fahrenheit what Kelvin is to Celsius. It is an absolute scale (starting at absolute zero) but it uses the exact degree size of Fahrenheit instead of Celsius. It is almost exclusively used by American aerospace engineers studying thermodynamics.
