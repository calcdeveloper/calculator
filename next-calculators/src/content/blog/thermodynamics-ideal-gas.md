## Manual Calculation Methods (Formulas)

While the digital calculator handles complex R-Constant matching and Kelvin conversions effortlessly, memorizing the core "PV=nRT" ecosystem is an absolute mandatory skill for any chemistry or engineering student passing their university exams.

### 1. The Core Formula (PV = nRT)

This is the ultimate law of theoretical gases.

**Pressure (P) x Volume (V) = Moles (n) x Gas Constant (R) x Temperature (T)**

**The R-Constant Rule:**
You MUST memorize the two major R-Constants. If you use the wrong one, your answer will be wildly, catastrophically incorrect.
*   If Pressure is in **Atmospheres (atm)** and Volume is in **Liters (L)**, you must use **R = 0.08206**.
*   If Pressure is in **Pascals (Pa)** and Volume is in **Cubic Meters (m3)**, you must use **R = 8.314**.

### 2. Solving for a Missing Variable (Algebra)

You simply isolate the variable you want on one side of the equals sign.

*   **To find Pressure (P):** P = (n x R x T) / V
*   **To find Volume (V):** V = (n x R x T) / P
*   **To find Moles (n):** n = (P x V) / (R x T)
*   **To find Temperature (T):** T = (P x V) / (n x R)

**Example Equation (Solving for Pressure):** You have 2 moles of gas in a 10-Liter tank at 300 Kelvin. What is the pressure in Atmospheres?
*   **Step 1 (Select R):** Because we want Atmospheres and we have Liters, R = 0.08206.
*   **Step 2 (The Numerator):** Multiply n x R x T (2 x 0.08206 x 300) = 49.236.
*   **Step 3 (Divide by Volume):** 49.236 / 10 = **4.92 Atmospheres**.
*   **Final Answer:** The tank is under nearly 5 times standard atmospheric pressure.

### The Absolute Temperature Trap (The Celsius Disaster)

This is the most critical rule in all of thermodynamics. **You cannot multiply or divide by Celsius or Fahrenheit.**

Why? Imagine you have a tank of gas sitting outside in the winter, and the temperature is exactly **0 degrees Celsius**.

The Celsius scale is arbitrary (based on freezing water). Thermodynamics requires an **Absolute Scale** based on physical molecular movement. That scale is **Kelvin**. Absolute Zero (0 Kelvin) is the theoretical temperature where all atomic vibration completely stops (-273.15 degrees Celsius).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

**1. What does "Ideal Gas" actually mean?**
An Ideal Gas is a theoretical physics concept. It assumes two things to make the math easy: First, the microscopic gas molecules take up zero physical volume. Second, the molecules never attract or repel each other (no intermolecular forces); they simply bounce off each other like perfect, frictionless billiard balls. 

**2. Are real gases actually Ideal?**
No. Real gas molecules do have a tiny bit of physical volume, and they do slightly attract each other. However, at normal room temperatures and standard Earth pressures, the molecules are so far apart and moving so fast that these tiny forces don't matter. The Ideal Gas Law is over 99% accurate for standard engineering.

**3. What is Avogadro's Law?**
It is a component of the Ideal Gas Law. It states that if you keep Temperature and Pressure locked, adding more Moles of gas will directly increase the Volume. Simply put: blowing more air (moles) into a balloon makes the balloon bigger (volume).

**4. What is Boyle's Law?**
Another core component. It states that if Temperature is locked, Pressure and Volume are inversely related. If you take a sealed syringe filled with air and violently crush the plunger down (decreasing the Volume), the internal Pressure will massively spike.

**5. What is Charles's Law?**
It states that if Pressure is locked, Volume and Temperature are directly related. If you take a balloon and put it in a hot oven (increasing Temperature), the balloon will physically expand (increasing Volume).

**6. Why is the R-Constant so confusing?**
Because the world cannot agree on a single measurement system. American engineers use PSI and cubic feet. European engineers use Pascals and cubic meters. Chemists love Atmospheres and Liters. Because the units change, the mathematical "glue" (the R-Constant) must change its numerical value to compensate and keep the equation perfectly balanced.

**7. How do I convert Grams to Moles?**
You must look at the Periodic Table of Elements. Find the atomic weight (molar mass) of your specific gas. For example, Nitrogen (N2) has a molar mass of 28 grams per mole. If you have 56 grams of Nitrogen gas, you divide 56 by 28 to get exactly 2 Moles.
