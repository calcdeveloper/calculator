## How does the Calculator work?

Because clock math relies on a Base-60 ceiling, calculating the true duration requires a complex "borrowing" algorithm, where borrowing 1 unit from the hours column yields exactly 60 units in the minutes column.

### Step 1: The 24-Hour Translation (Removing AM/PM)
If a user wants to calculate the time between 9:45 AM and 3:15 PM:
The calculator leaves the morning time alone (09:45).
It converts the afternoon time by adding 12 hours. (3:15 PM becomes 15:15).
It establishes a clean mathematical subtraction path. (15:15 minus 09:45).

### Step 2: The Base-60 Borrowing Algorithm
The calculator attempts to subtract the minutes: 15 minus 45. It fails because 15 is too small.
The calculator "borrows" exactly 1 Hour from the 15 hours. (15 hours becomes 14 hours).
It converts that 1 Hour into exactly 60 Minutes, and adds it to the 15 minutes. (15 + 60 = 75 minutes).
Now it executes the subtraction: 75 minus 45. (Remaining = 30 Minutes).
It executes the hour subtraction: 14 minus 9. (Remaining = 5 Hours).
It outputs the mathematically perfect duration. (Output: The duration is 5 Hours and 30 Minutes).

### Step 3: The Decimal Wage Conversion
If the user needs to bill a client for this time:
The calculator divides the remaining minutes (30) by 60. (30 / 60 = 0.5).
It attaches this decimal to the hours.
It outputs the strict financial billing metric. (Output: The billable time is 5.5 Hours).

## Manual Calculation Methods (Formulas)

While the digital calculator handles massive time blocks effortlessly, memorizing the manual "borrowing" algorithm is an incredibly useful life skill for filling out a timesheet when your phone battery is dead.

### The Manual "Base-60 Borrowing" Formula

Write the End Time on top (converted to 24-hour time), and your Start Time directly underneath it.

**Example:** Your shift started at 10:50 AM. You finished at 4:20 PM.

*   **Step 1 (Convert to 24-Hour):** Add 12 to the PM time. 4:20 PM becomes 16:20.
*   **Setup:**
    *   16 (Hours) : 20 (Minutes)
    *   10 (Hours) : 50 (Minutes)
*   **Step 2 (Subtract Minutes):** You cannot subtract 50 from 20. You must borrow 1 hour from the 16 (it becomes 15). Add exactly 60 minutes to the 20. (20 + 60 = 80). Now subtract: 80 - 50 = 30 Minutes.
*   **Step 3 (Subtract Hours):** Now subtract the remaining hours: 15 - 10 = 5 Hours.
*   **Final Answer:** You worked exactly **5 Hours and 30 Minutes**.

## Common Mistakes Users Make

When freelancers, HR professionals, and shift workers attempt these chronological calculations manually, they frequently fall into these catastrophic mathematical traps:

1.  **The "100 Minute Hour" Illusion:** This is the deadliest mistake in gig economy billing. If a freelancer works for 1 hour and 45 minutes, they often write "1.45" on their invoice. This is a massive error. 45 minutes divided by 60 is 0.75. The invoice should say "1.75 hours." Writing 1.45 cheats the freelancer out of a significant portion of their wage.
2.  **Borrowing Base-10 instead of Base-60:** When manually subtracting time (like in Step 2 of the manual guide), people often borrow "1" from the hours column and add "10" or "100" to the minutes column, because that is how normal math works. In clock math, borrowing 1 hour ALWAYS gives you exactly 60 minutes.
3.  **Confusing 12:00 PM with Midnight:** The transition from 11:59 AM to 12:00 PM (Noon) is highly confusing, as the numbers do not reset, but the AM/PM marker flips. Many people accidentally log 12:00 PM as midnight, completely ruining 12 hours of timesheet data. Always use the 24-hour clock method to prevent this.
4.  **Ignoring Unpaid Lunch Breaks:** When employees calculate their own paychecks, they often calculate the raw duration from clock-in to clock-out (e.g., 9 AM to 5 PM = 8 Hours). They forget that the company legally deducts the 30-minute lunch break, meaning the actual paid duration is 7.5 hours. Always subtract breaks before multiplying by your wage!

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

**1. Why does a clock use 60 minutes instead of 100?**
The modern clock is inherited from the ancient Sumerians and Babylonians, who used a Base-60 (Sexagesimal) counting system. The number 60 is mathematically beautiful because it can be divided evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, and 30, making fractional math (like "half an hour" or "a quarter of an hour") incredibly easy.

**2. How do I convert minutes into decimals for billing?**
You simply take the exact number of minutes and divide it by 60. For example, if you worked 15 minutes: 15 / 60 = 0.25. Your billable decimal is 0.25 hours.

**3. What is Military Time?**
Military Time (the 24-hour clock) removes the AM/PM confusion by continuing to count past 12. 1:00 PM becomes 13:00. 10:00 PM becomes 22:00. It is the absolute global standard for aviation, hospitals, and computer programming.

**4. How does the calculator handle crossing into a new day?**
